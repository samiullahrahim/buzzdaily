Nicolas Cage's Madden Is Now Streaming on Prime Video — and Critics Have Made It Christian Bale's Worst-Rated Film Ever

BuzzDaily Staff · Canada Desk
· 19 November 2026 · 8 min read
Photo: Guillaume Paumier, CC BY 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons · Nicolas Cage at WonderCon 2010 (file photo)

“Madden” arrived on Prime Video on Wednesday 18 November 2026 with the kind of reviews publicists have nightmares about. David O. Russell's biographical drama — Nicolas Cage as John Madden, Christian Bale as Al Davis — is now streaming worldwide, included with a Prime membership, and its 13% Rotten Tomatoes score has made it, by that measure, the worst-rated film of Christian Bale's nearly 40-year career.

The streaming launch follows a limited US theatrical run that began on 11 November and a Los Angeles world premiere on 4 November. Russell directed from his own script, reworking an earlier version by Cambron Clark. The film runs 110 minutes, is rated R, and reportedly cost $75 million to make. Its official trailer has been out since 24 September 2026 — long enough, it turns out, for curiosity about the two lead performances to build well ahead of the verdicts.

Coach, broadcaster, video-game empire

The film follows John Madden's improbable second and third acts: the Super Bowl-winning head coach of the Oakland Raiders who became the most famous voice in American sports broadcasting, and then the name on the video-game empire that still introduces new generations to the NFL. At the centre of the story is Madden's friendship with Raiders owner Al Davis — the relationship that made the coach, and the one the film keeps returning to as Madden moves from the sideline to the broadcast booth to a brand that long outgrew the sport itself.

Two transformations

Cage plays Madden with the physical commitment the role demands: he gained 25 pounds for the part and wore around 30 pounds of prosthetics and a fat suit. Bale, opposite him as Davis, is joined by Kathryn Hahn as Virginia Madden, Sienna Miller as Carol Davis, John Mulaney as Electronic Arts founder Trip Hawkins, Joel Murray as broadcaster Pat Summerall, and Shane Gillis.

13% — and a record Bale never wanted

The critical verdict landed before the streaming premiere did. “Madden” stands at 13% on Rotten Tomatoes from 15 reviews, having debuted at 15% from 13 reviews — a small sample, and the score may still move as more reviews arrive. On Metacritic it holds 33 from 13 critics, nine of them negative. Even allowing for the small numbers, the comparisons are stark: 13% makes “Madden” the lowest-rated film of Bale's career, below “Captain Corelli's Mandolin” at 28%. It is also the second collaboration between Russell and Bale to land in this territory, after “Amsterdam” — 32% on Rotten Tomatoes, a film that cost roughly $80 million and made $14 million domestically.

A pattern for Russell and Bale

For Bale, the number that stings is the career context: across nearly four decades of films, nothing he has headlined has scored lower — not even “Captain Corelli's Mandolin”, the 2001 wartime romance that sat at 28% and, until this week, held the unwanted title. For Russell, the pattern is more recent and more expensive. “Madden” is his second film with Bale, and the second to open to this kind of reception: “Amsterdam” managed 32% on Rotten Tomatoes and turned a budget of roughly $80 million into $14 million at the domestic box office. “Madden” reportedly cost $75 million — money that, this time, Amazon will try to recover in subscriptions rather than ticket sales.

What the critics actually said

The Associated Press's Jake Coyle gave the film 1.5 out of 4 and called it “a goofy, unfunny comedy that can't find the end zone”. The Hollywood Reporter's David Rooney called it a “cartoonish biodrama, led by the kind of stunt acting from both principals that never lets you forget they are Giving a Performance”. The A.V. Club and IndieWire both graded it D+. The dissent was narrow but real: Variety's Owen Gleiberman was the lone positive voice among the top critics, and Screen Rant gave the film 6/10, praising the chemistry between Cage and Bale as the heart of the film.

The split in the reviews is, at bottom, an argument about the acting. Rooney's charge — that both leads are visibly “Giving a Performance” — is the one most of the negative reviews circle back to, and Coyle's end-zone line makes the same point from the comedy side: a film this broad, they argue, keeps reminding you it is a film. The defence, such as it is, starts from the same place and reaches the opposite conclusion: Screen Rant's 6/10 review found the Cage–Bale chemistry to be the heart of the picture, and Gleiberman's positive notice for Variety suggests at least some critics thought the size of the performances matched the size of the men being played. Madden and Davis were not subtle figures, and the film was never going to be a subtle portrait of them.

Streaming changes the equation, though. A worldwide Prime Video premiere puts “Madden” in front of millions of subscribers who will never read a review, and its subject — a beloved coach whose name sits on one of the best-known game franchises in the world — gives it a built-in audience that a theatrical flop never gets to meet. Canadian viewers were already looking for it before the reviews settled: “madden movie” drew more than 20,000 searches in Canada, up 300%, with “john madden” also trending in Australia. Whether audiences side with the critics or with Gleiberman is now, quite literally, a one-click decision.
