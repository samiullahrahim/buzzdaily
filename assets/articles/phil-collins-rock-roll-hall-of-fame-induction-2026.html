Phil Collins Inducted Into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame as a Solo Artist as Lily, Joely and Nic Join His Rare Appearance

BuzzDaily Staff · US Desk
· 15 November 2026 · 8 min read
Photo: Raph_PH / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0) · Phil Collins performing at the Royal Albert Hall, London, in June 2017 (file photo) · Free licence — full author and licence details on the Commons file page

LOS ANGELES — Phil Collins is now a two-time Rock & Roll Hall of Famer. At the Peacock Theater in Los Angeles on Saturday night, 14 November, the 75-year-old was inducted into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame as a solo artist — a second induction that sits alongside the place he already holds in the Hall as a member of Genesis. He accepted the honour in a rare public appearance, walking with the aid of two canes, with three of his five children — daughters Lily Collins and Joely Collins and son Nicholas “Nic” Collins — at his side.

A SECOND INDUCTION, YEARS IN THE MAKING

Induction ceremonies run on career arithmetic, and Collins’s numbers have never really been in dispute. What made Saturday night land differently was the sight of the man himself. Collins’s health struggles are well documented, and public appearances have become rare enough that each one is an event in its own right. Arriving with two canes, he moved carefully through an evening built to celebrate him — and the image of the drummer who once seemed to power every record he touched, now taking the stage at a deliberate pace, gave the ceremony its emotional centre.

The solo induction also completes a particular kind of double. Collins was already a Hall of Famer with Genesis, the band he first joined as its drummer and later fronted. To be honoured again, alone, is the institution’s way of saying the second career was not an echo of the first. The solo records stood on their own — a different audience, a different sound, the same unmistakable voice and pulse.

THE SON BEHIND THE KIT

Of the three children beside him, Nic Collins carries the most literal connection to his father’s music. In recent years, as Collins’s own drumming has been affected by his health problems, Nic has played drums on tour with him — the son behind the kit, keeping the family rhythm while his father sang from the front of the stage. It is a handover few artists get to make in public, and it threaded quietly through Saturday’s appearance: the music continuing, one generation removed.

Lily Collins and Joely Collins completed the family group, and it was Lily whose name travelled furthest overnight. By Sunday, she had climbed into Canada’s Google Trends top 10, as photographs of the family’s red-carpet night spread and searches for the actress surged alongside the induction story — a neat measure of how a Hall of Fame night in Los Angeles now plays out in real time, through the family standing next to the inductee.

THE CLASS OF 2026

Collins was not inducted alone. The 2026 class also includes Billy Idol, Oasis, Sade, Iron Maiden, Wu-Tang Clan, Fela Kuti and Luther Vandross — a roll call that runs from punk and Britpop to soul, metal, hip-hop and Afrobeat, and one of the broadest classes the Hall has assembled. For readers following the metal wing of the class, BuzzDaily recently reported on Iron Maiden’s 50th anniversary celebrations in Auckland, where the fellow 2026 inductees stormed Spark Arena on their Run For Your Lives tour.

It is a class that invites comparison across generations of stadium music — the same lineage BuzzDaily traced when Celine Dion stole the show playing air bass at Metallica’s Sphere concert, another night when the legends of one era turned up to honour the noise of another. Collins, characteristically, let the songs make the argument.

THE RECORDS THAT EARNED IT

The case for the solo induction rests on a run of records that made Collins one of the best-selling solo artists of all time. “In the Air Tonight” remains the defining slow burn of the 1980s, a song whose drum break is still a cultural reflex. “Against All Odds” took his voice to the top of the charts as a ballad singer, and “Another Day in Paradise” showed the songwriter turning outward, towards the people the decade was leaving behind. Between the Genesis years and the solo years, there are very few corners of pop that his drumming, singing or writing did not reach.

That is why the second induction matters beyond ceremony. Plenty of artists are honoured for the band. Fewer force the institution to come back and reckon with what they did after it. Collins’s solo catalogue was not a side project; for much of the 1980s, it was the mainstream itself.

WHAT HAPPENS NEXT

Fans who were not at the Peacock Theater will not have to rely on photographs. The ceremony is due to air on ABC and Disney+ in December, bringing the induction — and the family moment at its heart — to the wider audience that has been following the pictures all weekend.

At 75, on two canes, flanked by three of his children and applauded by a class of inductees spanning five decades of popular music, Collins did not need to perform on Saturday night. The body of work had already done the performing. The Hall simply made it official — for the second time.
