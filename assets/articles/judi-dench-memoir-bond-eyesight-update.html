Dame Judi Dench at 91: Daniel Craig Wanted M Killed Off, a Rose Every Friday, and the Eyesight Update That Ended Her Acting Career

BuzzDaily Staff · UK Desk
· 15 November 2026 · 8 min read
Photo: Caroline Bonarde Ucci / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0) · Dame Judi Dench at the BAFTA Film Awards, London, in 2007 (file photo) · Free licence — full author and licence details on the Commons file page

LONDON — Dame Judi Dench is 91, and by every ordinary rule she should be beyond the reach of a news cycle. Instead she sits in Australia’s Google Trends top 10 today, 15 November, carried there by a new memoir that keeps giving up its secrets one headline at a time: that Daniel Craig campaigned to have her James Bond character M killed off, that her late husband sent her a rose every Friday for the whole of their marriage, and that the eyesight she has been losing since 2012 has now ended her acting career.

DANIEL CRAIG WANTED M KILLED OFF

In Is It Too Late to Make a Run for It?, released on 3 November and written with Brendan O’Hea, Dench reveals that Craig campaigned for M to be killed off in the Bond films — and that, in her account, it was Daniel’s idea to have M killed. Before that death could happen, she writes, she first had to suffer being “sacked” on screen by Ralph Fiennes’ character, the franchise clearing the desk before it closed the story.

It is the kind of revelation that rearranges how audiences remember an era. Dench’s M was the steely centre of the modern Bond films, and her exit was widely read as the producers’ decision. The memoir says the push came from the films’ star. The Bond succession debate has long since moved on — BuzzDaily has reported on Pierce Brosnan backing Callum Turner to be the next James Bond — but Dench’s account reopens the question of who really shaped the Craig era’s boldest choice.

THE LUNCH AT THE WOLSELEY

Dench also describes how she learned she was being let go: at a lunch at the Wolseley restaurant with producers Barbara Broccoli and Michael G. Wilson and director Sam Mendes. There is something very Bond about the setting — one of London’s grandest dining rooms, the franchise’s most powerful figures, and a decision delivered across a table. She tells the story without public bitterness, but she tells it precisely, names and all, which is its own kind of verdict.

MAGGIE SMITH, AND THE FEUD THAT WASN’T

The memoir also takes on one of the theatre world’s longest-running rumours: that Dench and her late friend Maggie Smith feuded. Dench dismisses talk that the two clashed over dressing-room status during the 2002 production of The Breath of Life, the play that put the two dames on stage together. Smith died in September 2024, and Dench admits she still misses her deeply. Those passages read less like a rebuttal than a tribute — two careers intertwined for decades, and a friendship the gossip never quite caught up with.

A ROSE EVERY FRIDAY

If the Bond passages make the headlines, the detail travelling furthest may be a quieter one. On The Graham Norton Show on BBC One on Friday 6 November, Dench revealed that her late husband, Michael Williams, sent her a rose every Friday of their marriage. They were married for thirty years less ten days before his death from lung cancer in 2001. A rose every Friday, for three decades: it is the sort of fact that explains a marriage better than any anniversary interview, and it has been shared and reshared all week.

“COMPLETELY BLOWN”: THE EYESIGHT UPDATE

The hardest passages concern her sight. In a CBS Sunday Morning interview aired on 8 November, Dench gave a stark update on the age-related macular degeneration she was diagnosed with in 2012. Her vision, she said, is “completely blown”: she can see only outlines, can no longer read or watch television, and has said she can no longer act on stage or in films.

For an actor whose instrument was the text — the precisely weighted line, the glance that lands a joke — the loss is total in professional terms, and she does not dress it up. What remains is the voice of the memoir itself: funny, unsentimental and exact. British screens have been shaped by leading ladies of Dench’s generation and the one that followed it — BuzzDaily recently charted Olivia Colman’s record-breaking run in Wicker — but Dench’s update marks the closing of a stage and screen career with few equals.

The memoir’s title asks whether it is too late to make a run for it. On this evidence, Dench is not running anywhere. She is doing something rarer at 91: setting the record straight, honouring the people she loved, and letting the work — seven decades of it — speak in her own words.
