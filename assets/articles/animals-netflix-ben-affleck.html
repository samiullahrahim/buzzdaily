Ben Affleck's Kidnapping Thriller “Animals” Is Streaming on Netflix — Reviews Are Negative Overall, but Gillian Anderson Draws Praise

By BuzzDaily Staff
· 11 October 2026 · 7 min read
Illustration: Original artwork created for BuzzDaily

Ben Affleck's newest directing project arrived on Netflix on Friday with a premise built for a weekend watch: a prominent Los Angeles family receives a late-night call saying its son has been kidnapped, and the price of bringing him home quickly grows larger than any ransom figure. Animals, now available to Netflix subscribers in the United States and the United Kingdom, is a 108-minute, R-rated crime thriller that uses the mechanics of an abduction story to examine a marriage, a political campaign and the bargains powerful people make when their carefully managed lives begin to collapse.

A crisis engineered to expose a family

Affleck plays Mark Kimball, a candidate for mayor of Los Angeles whose public identity depends on discipline, confidence and the appearance of domestic stability. Kerry Washington is Abigail Kimball, his wife and the other half of that polished image. When their son Denny, played by Christopher Woodley, is taken, the couple must raise money under intense time pressure while facing information they have kept from each other. The story's central tension is therefore not only whether they can save Denny, but whether the family that emerges from the ordeal will bear any resemblance to the one presented to voters.

That structure places Animals in recognizable Affleck territory. His films often focus on compromised adults trying to keep control of situations they helped create, and this one again treats institutions — politics, money, marriage and reputation — as systems that can protect people until the moment they become traps. The comparisons with Gone Girl are understandable: both films turn a marriage under public scrutiny into a mystery about performance and concealed resentment. But Animals is more direct and procedural, pushing its couple from one irreversible decision to the next rather than building its drama around an elaborate media puzzle.

Gillian Anderson changes the temperature

The most consistently praised element of the film is Gillian Anderson's performance as Dayle Burton, a fixer brought into the Kimballs' crisis. Dayle is useful because she understands how leverage works. She reads a room faster than the people who hired her, treats emotion as information and forces Mark and Abigail to confront the practical cost of their evasions. Anderson plays the role with controlled authority rather than spectacle; when Dayle enters a scene, the film becomes less about frantic reaction and more about who knows what, who can be trusted and who is prepared to act.

That precision helps explain why even a mixed review can single her out. Seat42F rated the film 6 out of 10, praising the ensemble and the moral territory while arguing that the urgency is uneven and that some complications arrive faster than the character work can support them. The wider critical response has also leaned negative. Those reservations matter because a thriller built around a ticking clock needs escalation to feel inevitable, not merely busy. Still, viewers who enjoy star-driven moral thrillers may find the performances and the pressure on the central marriage more compelling than the plotting.

A notable ensemble around the Kimballs

Steven Yeun plays campaign aide Sam, bringing the political operation into a family emergency that cannot remain private. Adriana Paz, Luis Gerardo Méndez and Ray Fisher round out a cast designed to keep shifting the audience's sense of loyalty and risk. Affleck co-wrote the screenplay with Connor O. McIntyre and Billy Ray. The production brings together Artists Equity, Makeready and Fifth Season, with Netflix distributing the film. Matt Damon is among the producers through Artists Equity, the company he co-founded with Affleck and Gerry Cardinale.

The project was once conceived as a more direct reunion for Affleck and Damon. Damon had been expected to lead the film, after Affleck directed him in Air, but scheduling around Christopher Nolan's The Odyssey changed the plan and Affleck stepped into the central role himself. The reshuffle also changed the on-screen couple, with Washington ultimately joining the film. That history adds an interesting layer to the finished result: instead of trading on the familiar chemistry between two lifelong collaborators, Animals asks Affleck and Washington to create a marriage whose public ease hides deep private instability.

The promotional joke — and a larger business conversation

The press campaign acknowledged Affleck and Damon's long association without pretending the original plan had survived. In a short promotional video posted to Washington's TikTok, she and Affleck recreated the best-known setup from Good Will Hunting, with Washington taking the place audiences would normally associate with Damon. The joke works because viewers understand the substitution immediately, but it also gives Washington a confident place in the campaign instead of treating her as an afterthought to a casting change.

Affleck's release-week interviews have also moved beyond the film. In Variety's 7 October cover story, he discussed why fewer performers now feel universally famous across the United States. His argument was not that contemporary actors lack talent; it was that studios no longer invest in building stars in the same concentrated way they did from the 1980s through the 2000s, while social-media reach has become part of modern casting and marketing decisions. He nevertheless pointed to Tom Holland, Jacob Elordi and Zendaya as current examples of widely recognized stars.

That conversation connects directly to Artists Equity. The company's model is built around sharing success with key creative participants rather than treating performance-based compensation as an exception. It also arrives as Affleck's relationship with Netflix expands beyond a single movie: the streamer recently acquired InterPositive, the AI filmmaking company he founded in 2022, and Affleck serves as a senior adviser. Those overlapping roles — filmmaker, performer, producer and technology adviser — make Animals more than a routine streaming release in his career. It is also a test of how his creative and business interests fit within Netflix's global platform.

Should you stream Animals this weekend?

For Netflix US and UK viewers deciding what to watch, the clearest recommendation is a qualified one. Animals is not arriving with a critical consensus behind it, and viewers looking for a perfectly calibrated kidnapping thriller may share the complaints about rhythm and overcomplication. But it offers an adult, performance-led alternative to franchise entertainment, with a compact running time, a strong cast and a subject that remains legible even as the plot becomes tangled: what happens when a family can no longer keep its private conduct separate from its public story?

Anderson is the strongest reason to press play, particularly for viewers who appreciate characters who dominate through intelligence rather than volume. Washington gives the domestic stakes weight, while Affleck's double duty as director and lead keeps the film closely aligned with his continuing interest in men whose authority begins to fail under pressure. The result is imperfect but discussable — the kind of streaming thriller whose best scenes may stay with viewers longer than its more mechanical turns.
