Vanity Fair’s ‘The Mastermind’ Paints a Dark Portrait of Taylor Swift — Fans Call It a Hit Piece

BuzzDaily Staff · US Desk
· 24 November 2026 · 9 min read
Photo: iHeartRadioCA / CC BY 3.0 via Wikimedia Commons · Taylor Swift at the Golden Globes, January 2024 (file photo)

NEW YORK — Vanity Fair has published one of the most talked-about celebrity profiles of the year, and it never once prints its subject’s name. ‘The Mastermind’, a long profile written by correspondent May Jeong and published on Monday, 23 November 2026, is built on interviews with more than 100 people — and throughout, Taylor Swift is referred to only as ‘The Star’ and ‘The Mastermind’. Other figures in her orbit receive nicknames of their own: Katy Perry, for example, appears as ‘The California Girl’. Within hours of publication the piece had become the entertainment story of the day, and the reaction split sharply. Many fans denounced it as a hit piece; some critics, looking at the same text, dismissed it as ‘weak tea’. Both readings are worth taking seriously, because the profile itself is built like an argument between two versions of the same life.

AN ALBUM IN TWO SIDES

Jeong structures the profile like a record. Its ‘A side’ sections tell the familiar version of Swift’s story — the small-town songwriter from Pennsylvania who became a billionaire, a champion of artists’ rights and the star of the highest-grossing tour in history. The ‘B side’ sections then offer a much less flattering account of the same events and the same character. The device lets the magazine hold both portraits at once, but it also means the harshest material sits in deliberate counterpoint to the official story, and readers are left to decide how much of the B side they believe. That question matters, because the profile’s most damaging claims come mostly from anonymous sources, while many of its named, on-the-record witnesses describe smaller, more ordinary unkindnesses — the gap between the two is where the argument over the piece now sits.

THE HARSH ANONYMOUS CLAIMS

The severest judgements in the piece are unattributed. A music industry executive, identified only as ‘The Exec’, tells Jeong of Swift: ‘She is punitive.’ Discussing her feuds, the profile concludes that ‘it’s truly Regina George’ — a reference to the queen bee of Mean Girls. Its central thesis is stated just as bluntly: ‘What emerges as The Star’s most enduring identity is not musician, not artist, not celebrity, but a combination of victim and victimizer.’ The piece says Swift ‘weaponizes her sense of victimhood’ and is willing to ‘crucify’ those who cross her. These are claims made by the profile and its anonymous sources, not established facts, and they should be read that way: Swift’s representatives were given the chance to respond, and the people making the strongest allegations did not put their names to them.

CLASSMATES SPEAK ON THE RECORD

The most concrete reporting in the profile comes from named former classmates at Wyomissing Area Junior/Senior High School in Pennsylvania, who spoke on the record about the singer’s schooldays. Chanice Skinner, who sat beside Swift in music class, said Swift was ‘always part of the popular crowd’ and that ‘if you weren’t part of her friend group, you didn’t exist to her’. Skinner also challenged Swift’s past accounts of having been bullied: ‘I was like, You were part of that. What are you talking about? If anything, you were one of the bullies.’ On the subject of the family’s move from Pennsylvania to Nashville in 2004 so that Swift could chase a music career, Skinner added: ‘We all had promise. It’s just that she had money.’ Arielle Peppers remembered Swift arriving at school in Ugg boots — ‘the brown one, the black one, the blue one’ — and recalled asking her own mother for a pair, only to learn that the cost came to half her mother’s paycheck. And Sharon Luyben disputed a story Swift told in a 2009 interview with Katie Couric, about friends moving their lunch trays away from her when she was about 12: ‘That wasn’t true. We had assigned seating in junior high.’

THE GUITAR TEACHER’S STORY

The profile also revives a claim from Swift’s former guitar teacher, Ronnie Cremer. According to Cremer, Andrea Swift brought home Taco Bell one day, offered the teenage Taylor a salad, and said: ‘Nobody wants to see a fat pop star.’ The anecdote is not new: Cremer first told the story to the New York Daily News in 2015, and Swift declined to comment on it then, just as she has now. It is one person’s recollection of a private moment, reported by the profile and repeated by Page Six, and it remains uncorroborated — a distinction the piece’s defenders and its critics have both, at times, glossed over.

FEUDS, A WEDDING AND A RAFFLE

The B sides run through Swift’s famous feuds, including the long saga with Katy Perry. Perry, ‘The California Girl’, is described in the piece as ‘an adult pretending to be a child’ — and the line is then turned back on Swift, who, the profile suggests, ‘might be a child pretending to be an adult’. The profile also covers Swift’s July wedding to Travis Kelce at Madison Square Garden and the couple’s $26 million donation to local charities beforehand, arguing: ‘Beyond need lies want. Beyond want, greed.’ It reports, too, a raffle at the wedding in which guests reportedly won a Chanel bag and a 1970 Chevrolet Chevelle. The wedding passage captures the profile’s method at its most debatable: the documented facts — a stadium wedding, a large charitable donation — are set against an interpretation that casts generosity itself as appetite.

NO COMMENT, AND A DIVIDED REACTION

Swift declined to comment when Vanity Fair put questions to her, and her representatives did not immediately answer requests from Page Six or the Daily Mail either. The profile itself notes how difficult the reporting was: many people refused to speak on the record, and one former collaborator advised Jeong to turn down the assignment. That context cuts both ways. For the piece’s defenders, the silence of so many insiders — and the reluctance even to be associated with the story — supports its portrait of a star whose displeasure people fear. For its critics, a case built largely on anonymous quotes and schoolyard memories from two decades ago, about a 36-year-old artist born in 1989 and raised in Reading, Pennsylvania, falls well short of the exposé its framing promises — hence the ‘weak tea’ verdict from some reviewers. What is not in dispute is the scale of the response: a nameless profile of the most famous pop star in the world became, within hours, the story everyone in entertainment was arguing about. Readers who want the public Swift rather than the profiled one can revisit BuzzDaily’s coverage of her Academy Museum Gala appearance — and judge the two portraits side by side.
