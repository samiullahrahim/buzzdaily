Dame Judi Dench ‘Can’t Wait’ to See Robert Pattinson as James Bond — as She Opens Up About Losing Her Sight

BuzzDaily Staff · Entertainment Desk
· 26 November 2026 · 5 min read
Photo: Caroline Bonarde Ucci / CC BY 3.0 via Wikimedia Commons · Dame Judi Dench at the BAFTA Film Awards, London, 2007 (file photo)

LONDON — Dame Judi Dench has given the new James Bond her blessing. Appearing on ITV’s Lorraine on Wednesday, 25 November 2026, alongside her close friend and writing partner Brendan O’Hea, the 91-year-old — who played MI6 chief M in seven Bond films — was asked about Robert Pattinson’s casting as 007. She said she “can’t wait” to see him in action, before adding the qualification that has defined her recent interviews: “Because I can’t see now, I can vaguely see and he looks terrific to me.” The appearance, to discuss her new memoir Is It Too Late to Make a Run for It?, mixed warmth about the franchise that made her a global star with unflinching honesty about the sight loss that has ended her acting career.

THE DEFINITIVE M GIVES HER VERDICT

Dench’s opinion carries a weight no other living actor’s can. She took over as M in GoldenEye in 1995 and held the role for seventeen years, across seven films, until her character was killed off in Skyfall in 2012 — one of the most consequential deaths in the franchise’s history. She was the first woman to play the role, and for a generation of viewers she simply is M. Her verdict on Pattinson therefore lands differently from ordinary celebrity endorsement. Amazon MGM Studios announced on 16 November 2026 that Pattinson, 40, will play Bond in the next film, directed by Denis Villeneuve and written by Steven Knight, ending a casting search in which Callum Turner, Jack Lowden, Josh O’Connor and Nicholas Hoult had all been reported as contenders — a race BuzzDaily followed from the Bond 26 screen tests to the announcement of Pattinson’s casting. Dench’s verdict — that he “looks terrific” — is the kind of approval the production could not have bought.

“I CAN’T READ ANYMORE”

The harder part of the Lorraine appearance was Dench’s account of her eyesight. She has age-related macular degeneration, diagnosed in 2012, a condition that destroys central vision. “I can’t read anymore, I can’t see the television. I can’t write any letters or anything,” she said. For an actor whose instrument was the text — who learned lines across six decades on stage and screen — the losses she lists are not inconveniences. They are the removal, one by one, of the tools of her trade and of much of her private life. She made the same point with even greater starkness on BBC Radio 4’s Today programme in October 2026: “I can’t be on stage. I can’t do a play anymore. I can’t do a film anymore. I can’t read anymore.” As BuzzDaily reported in its earlier coverage of Dench’s eyesight update, that October interview was widely understood as her farewell to acting. The Lorraine appearance, a month later, confirmed it in her own unsentimental terms.

“DO NOT GO GENTLE”

What Dench refuses to surrender is everything else. In an interview with Saga magazine published on 24 November 2026, she insisted that she will not retire, and will not “go quietly”: “I’m also worried that if I stop I’ll never start again: take the keys out of the ignition and the car will conk out. So no, don’t retire, do not go gentle and don’t even think about going quietly. The older you get, the louder you should sing.” The memoir itself is the proof of the philosophy — a book made with O’Hea, her collaborator and friend, by a woman who can no longer read a script but has found, in conversation and memory, another way to keep working. The Lorraine appearance had the same quality throughout: funny, precise, and entirely without self-pity.

WHY HER WORDS TRAVELLED

The reaction to the interview was immediate in the two markets where the story was always going to move. On Thursday, 26 November, “judi dench” was the number one entertainment search on Google Trends in Australia and number two in Canada — countries where her Bond films, and her decades of stage and screen work, built an audience that has aged alongside her. There is also, plainly, something about the combination she presented this week that audiences respond to: a 91-year-old, weeks from her 92nd birthday on 9 December, discussing the loss of her sight with total candour in one breath and, in the next, delivering a fan’s verdict on the new James Bond and a manifesto against retirement. The parts of her life that macular degeneration has taken are real, and she does not minimise them. The parts it has not taken were on full display on Wednesday morning television.

Dench’s M died in Skyfall, in one of the franchise’s most quoted scenes, and the role has since passed to a new generation — as, now, has Bond himself. Her blessing of the new man, formed from a vague impression and delivered with complete confidence, may be the most Judi Dench verdict imaginable: generous, funny, and entirely on her own terms. She can’t wait to see him, she says. On the evidence of this week, a great many people can’t wait to hear what she says next, either.
