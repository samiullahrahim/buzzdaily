Demi Lovato Gets Candid About Eating Disorder Recovery: 'My Worth Isn't in My Body'

BuzzDaily Staff · Entertainment Desk
· 16 October 2026 · 6 min read
Illustration: Original artwork created for BuzzDaily

Demi Lovato has spent most of her adult life being open about the things other pop stars hide — and on a new podcast episode, the 34-year-old delivered one of her most unguarded conversations yet about food, her body, and what recovery really looks like.

Appearing on Kerry Washington's "Street You Grew Up On," in an episode released October 14, Lovato told the actress she no longer thinks of her eating disorder as something she can fully leave behind. The singer, who has sought treatment multiple times over the years, described recovery not as a finish line but as an ongoing reality — one she has learned to manage.

"I've been to treatment several times," Lovato said, adding that she doesn't consider herself fully healed, and no longer expects to be. Food, she said, will be a battle for the rest of her life — but a manageable one.

It is the kind of candor that has defined Lovato's relationship with fans since her Disney Channel days, and it carries extra weight because of what came next: the story of her most recent tour.

The "It's Not That Deep Tour," which wrapped in May after nearly 20 shows, almost didn't get a confident Lovato on stage. She admitted she had gained weight before the run of dates, and the change triggered a wave of dread — about how she would look under the lights, and what strangers might say or "write online" about her appearance. "I was terrified," she recalled.

She performed anyway. And what happened in those rooms became, in her telling, one of the most healing experiences of her career.

Nobody walked out. Nobody jeered about her size. Instead, fans stayed in their seats, sang along, and cried tears of joy at her performances. Lovato said the response taught her something she had been trying to internalize for years: her value never had anything to do with a clothing size. She called the audience's embrace the most healing part of the tour.

That lesson has stayed with her. Lovato said she now lives "in a place of acceptance" — not loving her body every single day, but no longer looking in the mirror and berating herself for having curves. Cooking, once tangled up with food anxiety, has become a source of freedom and joy.

THE BACKSTORY

The openness is familiar territory for Lovato's fans. She broke out as a teenager on Disney Channel's "Camp Rock" and checked into inpatient treatment at just 18 to address physical and emotional struggles, including bulimia. Over the years she has spoken about how the disorder morphed — at one point into extreme dieting and over-exercising — and connected it to a 2018 relapse that ended in an overdose. Each time, she has insisted on making the private pain public so others feel less alone.

Earlier this year, Lovato released her first cookbook, "One Plate at a Time," a project aimed at people navigating disordered eating. In a March interview, she told fans struggling with their own relationship with food: "You deserve to fuel your body."

Marriage has become part of the foundation too. Lovato has been married to musician and songwriter Jordan "Jutes" Lutes since May 2025, and on the podcast she reflected that her husband doesn't love her for her body, her family doesn't love her for her body — she is, first, a good human being.

A TEXAS HOMECOMING

"Street You Grew Up On" walks celebrities back through their childhood homes, and Lovato's episode is steeped in Texas texture: growing up on Wilkes Drive in Colleyville, singing in her bedroom until sunrise, thrift-store pageant dresses, and a mother who once cheered for the Dallas Cowboys and shaped her early ambition. Lovato credits Kelly Clarkson with inspiring her own powerhouse voice, and traces the long road from pageants to "Barney & Friends" to "Camp Rock." The episode also touches on her work with the Elevate Prize and the family life she hopes to build one day. The show's own description carries a content note: this episode includes discussion of eating disorders and body image.

WHY IT MATTERS

What makes this interview stand out isn't just the admissions — it's the reframing. Celebrity recovery stories usually follow a tidy arc: crisis, treatment, triumph. Lovato refuses the arc. By calling full healing unrealistic, she offers a definition of recovery that is closer to how millions of people actually live it — a daily, manageable practice rather than a before-and-after story. And by locating her most healing moment not in a clinic but in a tour crowd that loved her at her least confident, she lands the point she has been making for more than a decade: acceptance from others can unlock acceptance of yourself.

For the fans who grew up alongside her — from "Camp Rock" tweens to adults now navigating the same pressures — that message may do more good than any triumphant finale ever could.
