Johnny Depp’s ‘Ebenezer’ Divides Critics as His Big Hollywood Comeback Hits Cinemas for Christmas

BuzzDaily Staff · Entertainment Desk
· 27 November 2026 · 6 min read
Photo: Harald Krichel / CC BY-SA 3.0 via Wikimedia Commons · Johnny Depp at the Berlinale, 2020 (file photo)

LONDON — Johnny Depp’s big Hollywood comeback has arrived in cinemas — and the critics cannot agree on what to make of it. ‘Ebenezer: A Christmas Carol’, the dark new adaptation of Charles Dickens’s 1843 novella, opened in cinemas on 13 November 2026, with Depp, 63, playing Ebenezer Scrooge in his first major studio lead role in years. Two weeks into the film’s run, the reviews have settled into a strikingly consistent pattern: Depp himself is largely praised as committed and entertaining, while the film around him is dismissed as vanilla and uninspired. It is a split verdict for a comeback that has been years in the making.

A COMEBACK YEARS IN THE MAKING

Depp’s return to the top of a studio cast list has been one of the most closely watched stories in film. His only significant role of the past six years was Louis XV in ‘Jeanne du Barry’, the little-seen French drama. Before that came the long legal battles with his ex-wife Amber Heard, which consumed his public life and coincided with his disappearance from major studio productions. ‘Ebenezer’, released by Paramount Pictures, is therefore more than a Christmas film: it is the first real test of whether one of the most bankable stars of his generation can still carry a big studio release — and the industry has been watching the answer as closely as the film itself.

A DARKER CHRISTMAS CAROL

The film comes from director Ti West, best known for the X trilogy and ‘Pearl’, working from a screenplay by Nathaniel Halpern based on Dickens’s novella. West’s background in horror shapes the adaptation: this is a dark-fantasy, horror-tinged ‘Christmas Carol’ set in Victorian London, leaning into the supernatural machinery of the story — the ghosts, the chains and the graveyard — rather than treating them as decoration. The film runs about 115 to 116 minutes, with music by Tyler Bates. It is, in other words, a Scrooge story told by a filmmaker whose instincts run to dread, released into the heart of the festive season.

A CHRISTMAS SLOT, A POINT TO PROVE

The timing is deliberate. A Dickens adaptation released on 13 November arrives at the very start of the Christmas season, when audiences are most receptive to the story and when a successful run can build week by week towards the holiday itself. For Paramount, the calculation is straightforward: Scrooge is one of the most recognisable characters in English literature, and Depp is one of the most recognisable actors alive. For Depp, the stakes are different. A seasonal hit would do what no interview or statement could — it would put the comeback on the scoreboard, in the only currency Hollywood fully trusts.

THE CAST AROUND SCROOGE

Depp is surrounded by a cast built to give the production weight. Ian McKellen plays Jacob Marley, Scrooge’s dead partner, whose return sets the haunting in motion. Rupert Grint plays Bob Cratchit, Scrooge’s clerk; Andrea Riseborough is the Ghost of Christmas Past; Tramell Tillman is the Ghost of Christmas Present; and Robert Strange plays the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come. Sam Claflin and Daisy Ridley also feature, with Ellie Bamber as Belle, the lost love of Scrooge’s youth. On paper it is an ensemble with the range to match West’s darker approach — part prestige Dickens, part ghost story.

WHAT THE CRITICS SAID

The reviews, when they came, were mixed to negative — but rarely about Depp alone. The A.V. Club headlined its verdict ‘Ebenezer review: Johnny Depp ruins Christmas’. The Times went with ‘Ebenezer review — Johnny Depp makes his bid for redemption’, and called the film uninspired. Frame Rated, reviewing on 23 November, argued that McKellen steals every scene he appears in, described Depp’s performance as campy and physical, and noted a genuine milestone: this is the first straight, gimmick-free live-action Dickens adaptation for the big screen since ‘A Christmas Carol’ in 1951. The pattern across the notices is hard to miss — admiration for the effort, reservations about the result.

TWO HEADLINES, ONE ARGUMENT

The two most quoted headlines of the film’s release tell the story of the whole comeback. To The A.V. Club, Depp ‘ruins Christmas’; to The Times, he is making ‘his bid for redemption’. Both are really reviewing the same thing — not just a performance, but a return. A quieter release might have been judged purely as a Dickens adaptation, the first straight one of its kind in 75 years. Instead, every notice has had to reckon with who Depp was, what happened to his career, and what his presence as Scrooge — a man offered the chance to change — is supposed to mean. The film’s divided reception is inseparable from that baggage, and Paramount’s Christmas bet is that audiences will want to judge for themselves.

THE VERDICT SO FAR

The consensus, two weeks in, is that Depp is committed and entertaining in parts, but that the film itself is vanilla and uninspired — a careful comeback vehicle rather than a bold one. Audiences, at least, are looking: ‘johnny depp ebenezer’ was the number one entertainment search in the UK and Canada on Google Trends and number two in Australia, with search volume up more than 1,000 per cent. Whether curiosity converts into a Christmas box-office run is the question the coming weeks will answer. What is already clear is that Depp has done the thing the comeback required of him: he has carried a studio film back into the centre of the conversation — even if the conversation is an argument.
