Molly Meldrum Dies Aged 83: Australia’s Countdown Legend and Champion of Local Music

BuzzDaily Staff · Australia Desk
· 23 November 2026 · 8 min read
Photo: Eva Rinaldi / CC BY-SA 2.0 via Wikimedia Commons · Molly Meldrum at the ARIA Awards, Sydney, 2014 (file photo)

MELBOURNE — Ian Alexander “Molly” Meldrum AM, the broadcaster who became the most recognisable champion of Australian music through ABC’s Countdown, has died aged 83. Meldrum died peacefully in the early hours of Monday, 23 November 2026, at Epworth Hospital in Richmond, Melbourne, after a long illness. His family confirmed the death in a statement, saying he died at 5:30am. No cause of death was given beyond the long illness described by the family. Within hours, his name was the most searched entertainment story in Australia and New Zealand — a final measure of a public affection that never really left him in six decades around the music he loved.

A STATE FUNERAL, AND A PRIME MINISTER’S TRIBUTE

Victoria will honour Meldrum with a state funeral. Victorian Premier Ben Carroll granted the state funeral at the family’s request — one of the highest honours the state can bestow, and a rare one for an entertainer. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese paid tribute to Meldrum on X, joining a wave of remembrance from across Australian public life. The tributes shared a common theme: gratitude. Artists, broadcasters and fans alike described a man who treated Australian music as something worth shouting about at a time when much of the local industry was still looking overseas for validation, and who kept shouting about it for the rest of his life.

THE COUNTDOWN YEARS

Meldrum’s fame rests above all on Countdown, the ABC pop programme that made him the conduit between Australia’s musicians and its teenagers. As the show’s host, he championed Australian and international artists alike — among them AC/DC, Kylie Minogue, INXS and ABBA — and his interviews and enthusiasms became events in themselves. For local acts, a Meldrum endorsement in the Countdown years meant prime-time exposure in living rooms across the country, often before the rest of the world had caught on. His famous sign-off, urging viewers to “do yourself a favour” and support the music he had just played, became part of the language — and, for a generation of Australian artists, it genuinely changed careers.

SIX DECADES AROUND MUSIC

Music was Meldrum’s whole working life. Across a career spanning six decades he worked as a music journalist, a record producer and a broadcaster, remaining a fixture of Australian entertainment long after Countdown ended. The industry formally recognised that contribution three times over: he was inducted into the ARIA Hall of Fame, the Logie Hall of Fame and the Music Victoria Hall of Fame. The hat became his trademark — he was rarely photographed without one — but colleagues consistently described the substance underneath the showmanship: an encyclopaedic knowledge of pop, and a loyalty to the artists he believed in that did not waver with fashion.

THE ACCIDENT THAT CHANGED HIS LATER YEARS

In 2011, Meldrum was seriously injured when he fell from a ladder at his home — an accident that made headlines around the world and began a long, closely followed recovery. He returned to public life afterwards, but more selectively, and his appearances in recent years were occasional rather than constant. The family’s statement on Monday asked for privacy, and gave no detail about his final illness beyond describing it as long. He had turned 83 in January, having been born on 29 January 1943.

FAMILY, AND WHAT HE LEAVES BEHIND

Meldrum is survived by his long-term partner Yan Wongngam, his family and his adopted son. What he leaves behind is harder to list but easy to see: a template for how a small country’s music can be talked up until the world listens, and a generation of Australian stars — from the pub-rock era to Kylie Minogue and INXS — whose first big break in front of a national audience came with Molly Meldrum introducing them. As BuzzDaily has reported in other recent farewells to music figures, including Love Affair’s Steve Ellis, the tributes tend to say the same thing in different words: the music survives, and so does the memory of the people who championed it.

The honours reflected a standing that went well beyond television. The “AM” after his name marked his appointment as a Member of the Order of Australia, a national recognition of the contribution the three hall-of-fame inductions also celebrated. For younger Australian musicians, he remained a reference point — the presenter against whom every later champion of local music has been measured, and a reminder that the country’s pop history was built as much by the people who promoted it as by the people who performed it.

A state funeral will give Australia a formal chance to say goodbye. But the informal farewell had already begun by Monday morning, in the playlists, the clips and the stories being shared by the fans he spent sixty years making. Do yourself a favour, he told them, every week. On the evidence of Monday’s reaction, they intend to return it.
