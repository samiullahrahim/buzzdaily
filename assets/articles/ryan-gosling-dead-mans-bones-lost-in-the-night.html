Ryan Gosling's Dead Man's Bones Return After 17 Years With 'Lost in the Night'

BuzzDaily Staff · Entertainment Desk
· 27 October 2026 · 10 min read
Photo: Dustin Drew · CC BY 2.0 · via Wikimedia Commons · Ryan Gosling performing with Dead Man's Bones at FYF Fest, Los Angeles, 2010 · Free licence — full author and licence details on the Commons file page (file photo)

LOS ANGELES — Some bands announce a comeback with a stadium tour and a documentary crew. Dead Man's Bones did it the way they have always done things: with a ghost story, a child's voice and a date whispered out of the dark. On Tuesday, 27 October, Ryan Gosling and Zach Shields released “Lost in the Night” through ANTI- Records — the first new Dead Man's Bones music in seventeen years, arriving four days before Halloween, exactly where a band obsessed with haunted houses, graveyards and things that go bump in the night was always going to resurface.

The return was set in motion on 22 October, when the duo posted a short teaser video to Instagram. A man's face floats in a slow, sinister spiral while a voice recites a poem about ghosts preparing to leave their world — “Where winds are black as midnight breath. Where hands can't find the light to loose, the chains that keep me far from you” — before a young girl's voice breaks in to announce: “Oct. 27!” The clip, also shared by ANTI- under the title “Hypnotism”, was the duo's first public sign of life since the brief run of shows that followed their 2009 debut album. By Tuesday morning the song itself was out, accompanied by a black-and-white video, and the seventeen-year silence was officially over.

A SONG RECORDED IN A DAY, ON A FARM

The most striking detail of the comeback is how the song was made. According to the announcement materials, Gosling and Shields first tried to record at several Los Angeles studios and could not find the sound they wanted. So they left the city, took two guitars, two drum machines and two synthesizers to a farm outside Los Angeles, and tracked “Lost in the Night” in a single day. The single is produced by Gosling and Shields with longtime collaborator Tim Anderson as executive producer, and it is released by ANTI-, the same independent label that issued the duo's debut in 2009.

The result, as Stereogum described it on release day, is reverb-heavy, atmospheric goth-rock — a notable shift from the ghostly children's-choir folk of the debut. There is no children's choir this time. Instead there are drum machines, glassy synthesizers, echoing harmonies and the chirp of crickets running underneath, with Gosling murmuring the lead vocal through heavy echo. The press materials name Bruce Springsteen and the New York duo Suicide as touchpoints. It is, unmistakably, a Halloween record — but one made by two men now deep into their forties, and it sounds like it: less a séance in a school hall, more a drive through the desert at 2 a.m. with the radio half-tuned to something old.

A VIDEO WITHOUT THE BAND IN IT

True to the project's theatrical instincts, neither Gosling nor Shields appears in the “Lost in the Night” video — or if they do, they are hidden well in the background. The black-and-white clip, which carries no director's credit, stars Noah Jupe, known to audiences from the first two “A Quiet Place” films, and Rain Spencer, of “The Summer I Turned Pretty” and the indie drama “Good Girl Jane”. It follows a young couple from an above-ground graveyard into a romantic nighttime adventure — a short film in miniature, and a reminder that Dead Man's Bones was always as much a visual project as a musical one. The accompanying press photograph makes the point with a wink: Gosling dressed as Frankenstein's monster, Shields as a Wolfman.

THE STRANGEST DEBUT OF 2009

To understand why a single song after seventeen years is news, it helps to remember how unlikely the band's first life was. Gosling and Shields formed Dead Man's Bones in 2007, in the space between Gosling's indie-film turns in “Lars and the Real Girl” and “Blue Valentine”, after bonding over a shared childhood obsession with Disneyland's Haunted Mansion ride. Shields, who has since built a screenwriting career on films including “Krampus” and the recent American Godzilla pictures, shared Gosling's taste for the playfully morbid — Disney's “Skeleton Dance”, old horror films, and a cult album of schoolchildren singing pop songs that had resurfaced in the early 2000s.

Their self-titled debut, released by ANTI- in 2009, was recorded with the Silverlake Conservatory Children's Choir, whose young voices drift through songs about ghosts, monsters and buried treasure. The pair set out to play the instruments themselves, learning as they went, and the album's homemade eeriness became its charm. Rolling Stone's verdict at the time — “Arcade Fire, Tom Waits and the cast of Sesame Street performing a Kurt Weill musical” — has followed the record ever since. The band toured briefly around Halloween 2009, with a handful of further shows in 2010, performing alongside the costumed choir. Then Gosling's film career went stratospheric, Shields' writing career took off, and Dead Man's Bones became the most beloved footnote in both men's biographies.

EVA MENDES SAW IT COMING

If anyone can claim to have summoned this comeback, it is Eva Mendes. In the days before the 22 October announcement, Gosling's partner was posting old horror-movie footage cued to the debut album's “In the Room Where You Sleep”, and sharing a video of herself walking through a grove of trees to Gosling's spectral singing, captioned: “Trying to summon new music from DEAD MAN'S BONES. I can feel it in the air…” When a fan asked whether she knew something they didn't, she replied, “Maybe I do…” After the teaser dropped, she reposted it with the caption that has since been quoted everywhere: “He came through on his promise to me.” It is a small detail, but a telling one — this was not a label-driven revival. By every account, the project was kept secret simply because two old friends wanted to make it, and did.

THE MOVIE STAR WHO NEVER STOPPED SINGING

Gosling, of course, never actually stopped performing music — he just did it inside films. He played the jazz pianist Sebastian in Damien Chazelle's “La La Land” in 2016, singing “City of Stars” and winning the Golden Globe for Best Actor in a Musical or Comedy. In 2023 he stole scenes in Greta Gerwig's “Barbie” with the power ballad “I'm Just Ken”, which carried him all the way to a live performance at the 2024 Academy Awards and a Grammy nomination. His most recent picture, “Project Hail Mary”, is one of the biggest releases of 2026, and his schedule ahead — a Star Wars film, “Starfighter”, among it — is that of about as mainstream a movie star as exists. Which is precisely what makes the Dead Man's Bones return land: it cannot be explained by money, profile or career strategy. A one-day recording on a farm, released on an independent label, under a name most casual fans have never heard, is the opposite of all three.

WHAT HAS NOT BEEN ANNOUNCED

For now, “Lost in the Night” is a single song, and readers should hold the rest lightly. As of release day there is no confirmation of a second Dead Man's Bones album, no announced tour and no live dates — and given that the duo's last performances were in 2010, and that both men have somewhat demanding day jobs, any speculation about concerts is exactly that. What has been confirmed, by the label and the release itself, is narrower and better: the song exists, it is out now on ANTI-, and it credits Gosling and Shields as composers, lyricists and producers. BuzzDaily will report any album or live news only when the band or the label confirms it.

ANALYSIS: THE COMEBACK NOBODY HAD TO MANUFACTURE

Analysis — the music industry runs on reunion economics now: anniversary tours, legacy-act residencies, catalogue sales. Dead Man's Bones fits none of it, which may be why the reaction since 22 October has been so uniformly warm. There was no catalogue to speak of — one album, seventeen years ago, that sold modestly and grew in affection the longer it stayed buried. The teaser asked for nothing but attention, the song arrived on time and on theme, and the whole thing was over, in a sense, in a day of recording. For US audiences scrolling past a dozen manufactured “surprise” drops this autumn, that restraint is the story. For UK listeners who discovered the debut through late-night radio and Halloween playlists, it is simply a pleasure to have the strangest band in Hollywood back in the season they belong to.

Seventeen years is a long time to wait for a second song. But Dead Man's Bones were never really a band on the industry's clock — they were two friends making ghost music because the Haunted Mansion scared them as children and delighted them as adults. “Lost in the Night” suggests the friendship, and the fascination, survived everything fame did in between. The bones, it turns out, were only resting.
