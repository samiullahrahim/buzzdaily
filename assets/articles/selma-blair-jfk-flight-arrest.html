Selma Blair Arrested After Alleged Delta Flight Disturbance; Pleads Not Guilty as FBI Investigates

BuzzDaily Staff · Entertainment Desk
· 15 October 2026 · 8 min read
Photo: Selma Blair at the 2026 Vanity Fair Oscar Party, via The Blast

Selma Blair is facing scrutiny in two legal systems after a disturbance aboard a Delta Air Lines flight from Los Angeles to New York ended with her arrest at John F. Kennedy International Airport. The actor has pleaded not guilty to two state assault charges connected to what prosecutors say happened after the plane landed, while federal authorities are separately examining the alleged conduct in the cabin.

The case began late Sunday, October 11, when Port Authority police and FBI personnel were sent to Delta Flight 938 after a report of a physically abusive passenger. The Port Authority identified Blair, 54, as the person taken into custody when the aircraft reached its gate. Two flight attendants reported injuries, and one was taken to a hospital for further evaluation. Blair was also transported to a hospital for medical assessment.

Reporting about the events onboard remains partly allegation and partly official account. TMZ reported that Blair allegedly tried to open an aircraft door during the flight and that one crew member was struck while another was bitten. USA Today reported that Delta was aware of the door allegation but could not independently corroborate it. CNN, citing a law-enforcement official, reported that one attendant had been bitten and another suffered a cut. No court has determined that Blair committed any of those acts.

Delta said the flight itself was not operationally disrupted and arrived ahead of schedule. The airline confirmed that law enforcement met the aircraft and emphasized that customer and crew safety comes first. The FBI, which investigates alleged assaults aboard aircraft, confirmed that it was reviewing the incident but said it would not discuss an active investigation.

A MEDICAL EMERGENCY, ACCORDING TO HER REPRESENTATIVE

Blair’s representative, Jill Fritzo, offered a different but not necessarily competing account of the episode: “Selma experienced a medical emergency that led to the alleged conduct. Upon landing, she received immediate medical attention and is currently in stable condition.” The statement did not specify the nature of the emergency.

At Blair’s arraignment the next day, her lawyer, Shawn Barnes, told the court that she had worked long hours before traveling, had missed several doses of prescribed medication and had fallen and hit her head during the flight. He argued that she became severely confused and disoriented and that the alleged behavior was not intentional. Those claims are part of the defense account and have not yet been tested through evidence in court.

Blair disclosed in 2018 that she had been diagnosed with multiple sclerosis and has since become a prominent advocate for people living with disabilities and chronic illness. Her attorney also referred in court to neurological symptoms. But neither the public statement nor the reporting available so far establishes that multiple sclerosis caused what happened on the flight. Treating a known diagnosis as a complete explanation would go beyond the evidence and risk reducing a developing legal and medical situation to speculation.

The distinction matters because compassion and accountability are not mutually exclusive. Injured crew members, an officer and a nurse are entitled to have their allegations taken seriously. Blair is also entitled to medical privacy, a presumption of innocence and the opportunity to present evidence about her condition and state of mind. Responsible coverage should hold all of those facts at once rather than turn a medical crisis into entertainment.

WHY THERE ARE TWO DIFFERENT LEGAL TRACKS

The two state charges filed in Queens Criminal Court do not arise directly from the allegations involving the flight attendants. According to the criminal complaint described by CNN, prosecutors say Blair kicked a Port Authority police officer while being secured to a stretcher and later kicked a nurse at the hospital. She pleaded not guilty to two counts of second-degree assault. A judge set bail at $20,000 cash or $60,000 bond, and Blair was released after posting bail. The court also issued an order of protection involving the nurse.

The alleged behavior aboard the aircraft is being handled separately because crimes on commercial flights can fall within federal jurisdiction. CNN reported that the FBI issued Blair a summons related to alleged assault on the flight. A summons starts a legal process; it is not a conviction, and the precise federal schedule had not been publicly set in the reporting reviewed for this article.

That division can be easy to miss in fast-moving celebrity coverage. The New York case concerns the alleged post-landing encounters with the officer and nurse. The federal matter concerns what authorities say happened while the aircraft was in flight. Evidence, potential defenses and charging decisions may therefore develop on different timelines.

Blair’s next reported appearance in the state case is scheduled for January 19, 2027. Before then, prosecutors and defense lawyers may exchange records, medical information, witness statements and other evidence. The FBI can continue interviewing passengers and crew, reviewing dispatch communications and determining whether the federal allegations should move forward. Any final judgment belongs to the courts, not headlines or social media.

THE 2016 FLIGHT INCIDENT — AND THE LIMITS OF THE COMPARISON

The arrest has revived coverage of a separate incident from June 2016, when Blair was removed on a stretcher from a flight from Cancún to Los Angeles and hospitalized after an outburst. She later said she had mixed alcohol with medication, blacked out and behaved in a way she regretted. She apologized to the passengers and crew and described the episode as a serious mistake.

The earlier event is relevant background because Blair addressed it publicly and because both situations involved medical care after a disturbance on a plane. It is not proof of what happened in 2026, however. The two episodes occurred a decade apart, under different circumstances, and there is no verified evidence that they share the same cause. The current case must be evaluated on its own facts.

Blair’s health journey has been unusually public. Since announcing her multiple-sclerosis diagnosis, she has spoken candidly about mobility, fatigue, treatment and the value of disability visibility. That history makes the representative’s medical-emergency statement significant, but it does not remove the need for a careful investigation. Nor should the investigation become an invitation to mock neurological symptoms or speculate about treatment details that have not been disclosed.

For now, the clearest account is also the most measured one: a flight crew reported a serious disturbance; several people said they were injured; Blair received immediate medical attention; her defense says she was experiencing a crisis; and state and federal proceedings remain unresolved. The next meaningful developments will come from verified court records and official investigative updates, not from assumptions about either guilt or illness.
