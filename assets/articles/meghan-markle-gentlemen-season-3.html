Meghan Markle Out of The Gentlemen Season 3 Running as Netflix Casting Saga Takes Final Turn

BuzzDaily Staff · Entertainment Desk
· 18 October 2026 · 7 min read

LONDON — The casting saga that has shadowed Guy Ritchie's Netflix hit The Gentlemen for more than a year appears to have reached its final act. Meghan Markle, the Duchess of Sussex, is officially out of the running for a role in the show's third season, according to new reports — with friends of the duchess telling The Telegraph she feels she was "used to help promote" the show's second season, and The Sun reporting that director Guy Ritchie responded with a blunt "not a chance" when her name was raised.

It is a definitive ending to months of speculation that linked one of the most famous women in the world to one of television's most talked-about crime dramas. And for a show that has quietly become one of Netflix's steadiest performers on both sides of the Atlantic, the episode says as much about the streaming era's marketing machinery as it does about casting.

What happened, exactly — and why does a casting decision on a crime caper keep making headlines? Here is the story, step by step.

The Timeline: How the Rumor Became a Saga

The whispers began long before The Gentlemen Season 2 arrived. In the first months of 2026, entertainment reporters on both sides of the Atlantic began asking the obvious question: with Season 1 a certified hit, who would join the expanding world of Theo James's Eddie Horniman and Kaya Scodelario's Susie Glass?

Markle's name surfaced early and persistently. The reasons were not hard to see. She is a former television actress — Suits made her a recognizable face in American living rooms for seven seasons — now living in California but retaining enormous cultural visibility in Britain. She has an ongoing relationship with Netflix through her and Prince Harry's production company. And Guy Ritchie, a director whose brand is built on swagger and star power, would be an obvious match for the kind of glossy, high-wattage casting the streaming service loves.

Through the spring, the chatter intensified. Reports described conversations, meetings, and interest — the familiar language of a casting story that is real enough to report but never quite confirmed. Netflix, following its usual policy, declined to comment. Markle's representatives were equally quiet. The vacuum filled itself.

Then came September 3, 2026, and the premiere of Season 2 — without Meghan Markle. The new season, reuniting James and Scodelario, drew solid reviews and strong viewing figures. But the question of the duchess's involvement had not gone away; if anything, the premiere sharpened it, because reports began suggesting that her name had been floated publicly in ways that served the show's promotion more than any genuine casting process.

This week's reports appear to close the book. Friends of the duchess told The Telegraph that "some people in her camp feel she was used" — that talk of her joining was amplified "because they knew it wasn't as good as the first season," and that the episode amounted to "the oldest marketing trick in the book." Separately, The Sun reported that when Markle's name came up in connection with the show, Ritchie himself responded: "Not a chance."

Neither Netflix nor Ritchie's representatives have issued formal statements, and the duchess's camp has not commented beyond the friends quoted in the reports. But the direction of travel is clear: Season 3 will go ahead without her.

The "Used" Claim: What It Really Means

The most striking element of this week's reporting is not the casting decision itself — actors are considered and passed over every day in Hollywood — but the accusation embedded in it: that Markle's name was deployed as a marketing instrument.

The friends quoted by The Telegraph describe a familiar pattern in the streaming era. A famous name is attached, loosely, to a project. Entertainment media — always hungry for a story that combines royalty, Hollywood, and Netflix — amplifies it. The project gets weeks of free publicity. And when the season finally arrives, the famous name is nowhere to be found, but the attention has already been banked.

Whether that is what happened here is, strictly speaking, unproven. No one has produced evidence of a deliberate strategy, and it is entirely possible that genuine early conversations simply did not progress — the most ordinary outcome in casting. Interest at the development stage routinely evaporates once scripts, schedules, and creative direction firm up. Ritchie's reported "not a chance" remark, if accurate, may reflect nothing more than a director protecting the tone of his show.

But the perception matters, particularly for someone in Markle's position. Since stepping back from royal duties, the Duchess of Sussex has worked carefully to rebuild her professional identity — as a producer, a podcast host, and a lifestyle brand founder. Being seen as a pawn in someone else's marketing campaign cuts directly against that project. The Telegraph's framing — friends speaking on her behalf, the language of being "used" — reads as an attempt to reclaim the narrative: she was not rejected; she was exploited, and she is walking away.

It is worth noting, too, that this is not the first time the Sussexes' Netflix relationship has generated this kind of friction. Their partnership with the streamer has produced genuine hits — the Harry & Meghan docuseries remains one of Netflix's most-watched documentaries — but it has also produced its share of tabloid turbulence. Every project announcement is dissected; every delay is a story. In that environment, even a routine casting non-event becomes a saga.

Netflix's Playbook: Why Big Names Matter

To understand why the Markle rumors had such staying power, it helps to understand what Netflix is selling with The Gentlemen — and what a name like hers would have been worth.

The Gentlemen began life as a 2019 Guy Ritchie film, a return-to-form crime caper that grossed over $100 million worldwide. The Netflix series, launched in 2024, expanded the world: Theo James as Eddie Horniman, the reluctant heir to a cannabis empire, and Kaya Scodelario as Susie Glass, the razor-sharp operator who may be the smartest person in any room she enters. Season 1 was a genuine phenomenon — Netflix's data put it among the most-watched English-language series of its debut window, and it performed strongly in both the United States and the United Kingdom, a transatlantic double that few shows manage.

Season 2, which premiered September 3, faced the classic sequel problem: how to keep the energy without repeating the formula. Reviews were respectable rather than rapturous, and while viewing figures held up, the cultural conversation was quieter. In that context, the value of a headline-grabbing casting rumor is obvious. A story linking Meghan Markle to the show generates coverage in outlets that would never otherwise write about a crime drama's second season — royal correspondents, American morning shows, lifestyle press. It is, as the Telegraph's source put it, the oldest trick in the book, precisely because it works.

This is not unique to Netflix, and it is not new. Studios have floated famous names for projects since the studio era. What has changed is the speed and scale: a single unsourced report can now circle the globe in hours, generating millions of impressions before anyone has confirmed a thing. For streamers competing for attention in an impossibly crowded market, the temptation to let a rumor run is enormous.

The question — one the industry will be asking after this episode — is whether the short-term gain is worth the long-term cost. If high-profile talent begins to feel that their names are being used as marketing confetti, they may become warier of engaging with streamers at all. Trust, in Hollywood as everywhere else, is a currency.

Season 3: What We Actually Know

Stripped of the saga, the substantive news is straightforward: The Gentlemen is moving ahead with a third season, and its casting is taking shape without Meghan Markle.

The most notable new addition reported for Season 3 is Robert Carlyle, the Trainspotting and Once Upon a Time star, whose casting brings heavyweight dramatic credentials to Ritchie's ensemble. Carlyle, a Scottish actor with decades of acclaimed work on both sides of the Atlantic, is precisely the kind of performer who fits the show's world: charismatic, dangerous, and capable of stealing scenes from anyone.

Also expected to feature more prominently is Maya Jama, the British television presenter whose profile has risen sharply in recent years. A bigger role for Jama would continue the show's pattern of blending established dramatic actors with personalities drawn from elsewhere in British popular culture — a Ritchie signature.

James and Scodelario, the show's anchors, are expected to return, though Netflix has not confirmed full casting details. Production timelines have not been announced, but given the September 2026 premiere of Season 2, a late-2027 or early-2028 arrival for Season 3 would fit the established rhythm.

For fans, the takeaway is simple: the show goes on, with its core intact and new blood on the way. The Markle episode, however noisy, was always peripheral to what makes The Gentlemen work — Ritchie's direction, the James-Scodelario chemistry, and a world of aristocratic criminals that viewers on both sides of the Atlantic have embraced.

The Bigger Picture: Royalty, Hollywood, and the Attention Economy

There is a reason this story traveled so far, and it has little to do with television casting. Meghan Markle occupies a unique position in the culture: a former actress turned duchess turned California media entrepreneur, she is simultaneously a royal story, a Hollywood story, and a business story. Any report involving her activates all three news ecosystems at once.

That is why a casting rumor that would barely register for another actor becomes a multi-month saga for her — and why both Netflix and the duchess's camp have incentives to manage the narrative carefully. For Netflix, the association is pure upside: prestige, attention, transatlantic relevance. For Markle, the calculus is more delicate. Every project she attaches her name to is scrutinized for what it says about her post-royal identity. A splashy role in a hit crime series could have been a triumphant acting comeback; a protracted will-she-won't-she that ends with "not a chance" is something else entirely.

The neutral reading of this week's reports is that both sides are simply moving on. Ritchie has his Season 3 cast to assemble. Markle has her own slate — her lifestyle brand, her production projects, her family life in Montecito. The entertainment industry is full of near-misses; most are never reported at all.

But the "used" allegation gives the story a sharper edge, and it will linger. If true, it suggests a coarsening of the already blurry line between casting news and marketing strategy. If overstated, it still reveals how keenly the duchess's camp polices her public image — and how quickly a routine Hollywood process can curdle when one of the most watched women in the world is involved.

Either way, the saga is over. Season 3 of The Gentlemen will arrive with Robert Carlyle, with Maya Jama in a bigger role, and with the continuing adventures of Eddie Horniman and Susie Glass. Meghan Markle will not be among the cast. And the next time a famous name is linked to a streaming hit, audiences on both sides of the Atlantic may read the headlines with a little more skepticism — which, given how this one played out, is probably for the best.
