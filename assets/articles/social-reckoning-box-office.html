Sony's “The Social Reckoning” Bombs With $5.5 Million Opening Weekend as Jessica Chastain's Horror “Other Mommy” Takes Box Office Crown

BuzzDaily Staff · Entertainment Desk
· 11 October 2026 · 7 min read
Illustration: Original artwork created for BuzzDaily

Hollywood had high hopes for Aaron Sorkin's return to the world of Facebook. Instead, Sony's The Social Reckoning limped into theaters this weekend with a dismal $5.5 million domestic opening — landing in fifth place and instantly ranking as one of the biggest disappointments of the 2026 movie year.

The film, which dramatizes whistleblower Frances Haugen's 2021 disclosures about Facebook's internal research on algorithmic engagement and teen harm, arrived sixteen years after Sorkin's Oscar-winning The Social Network. That 2010 classic opened to $22.6 million on its way to becoming one of the defining films of its era. This time, audiences simply did not show up.

A lopsided weekend at the multiplex

By comparison, Universal and Blumhouse's horror newcomer Other Mommy dominated the frame with a $27.5 million debut. The film stars Jessica Chastain as a mother who discovers a sinister doppelganger is haunting her daughter, and it added another $17.6 million overseas for a $45 million global start — all on a reported production budget of just $18 million to $19 million.

The numbers tell a brutal story. The Social Reckoning played in 2,606 theaters yet grossed less than a third of what its famous predecessor earned in its first weekend. According to industry estimates, the film's production budget ran to roughly $30 million to $40 million after incentives, and with just $3.5 million from 29 overseas markets, its worldwide total sits around $9 million — a start that leaves the film far short of profitability.

Warning signs before release

What went wrong? The warning signs were there before release. Pre-release tracking estimated a $7 million opening, and Thursday previews brought in only about $650,000, followed by an estimated $2.3 million Friday. Analysts point to several compounding problems. The film's marketing leaned heavily on its connection to The Social Network, but many moviegoers felt the comparison hurt more than it helped. And the subject matter — a dramatization of the January 6 riots and Facebook's documented struggles with teen safety — may have felt more like a history lesson than entertainment to audiences already living through those events.

The casting also drew scrutiny. Jeremy Strong stepped into the role of Mark Zuckerberg after Jesse Eisenberg declined to return, a decision that many reviewers and viewers found distracting. Critics, however, were kinder to the supporting cast: Mikey Madison, playing whistleblower Frances Haugen, and Jeremy Allen White, playing Wall Street Journal reporter Jeff Horwitz, earned consistent praise for their performances. The criticism fell squarely on Sorkin's direction and the film's pacing and tone, with several reviews describing it as a generic journalistic potboiler rather than the sharp drama audiences expected.

Sony's marketing campaign tried to lean into the controversy. The studio ran a pointed Instagram ad that directly referenced Zuckerberg and the platform's advertising policies, and the production underwent legal vetting with outreach to real people portrayed in the film. Sorkin himself made the press rounds, telling Morning Joe that everyone should be concerned about Meta's power, calling it “incredibly dangerous,” and warning that tech billionaires were “palling around” with political power. None of it moved the needle at the ticket window.

A year of extremes for Sony

The irony for Sony is especially sharp. Earlier this year, the studio enjoyed the biggest hit of 2026 with Spider-Man: Brand New Day, which grossed more than $2 billion worldwide. Now it holds both the biggest hit and the biggest flop of the year, after Tom Cruise's Digger — another expensive disappointment sitting at just $14.4 million domestic and $35.3 million worldwide against a production budget north of $160 million.

Other Mommy wins despite a harsh verdict

The weekend's other story was Other Mommy, which won the frame despite terrible reviews. The horror film scored a D+ on CinemaScore exit polls — the lowest grade recorded by the audience poll since Francis Ford Coppola's Megalopolis two years ago — and sits at just 19% on Rotten Tomatoes. Even Digger managed a C+ from polled audiences last weekend.

Horror movies are traditionally resilient to bad reviews, but analysts say this extreme case will test that rule. Box-office analyst David A. Gross, who publishes the FranchiseRe newsletter, called the reception “extreme” and predicted “business is going to drop sharply” in the coming weeks. With Halloween approaching, competition from the strong holdover Resident Evil and DC Studios' upcoming Clayface will not help.

Still, Other Mommy is well positioned financially. On its modest budget, the $45 million global start should let it clear its break-even point theatrically even if domestic grosses fall fast. That is more than can be said for The Social Reckoning, which — on a roughly $30 million to $40 million budget — faces what Deadline's box-office coverage described as a likely “bomb” with limited room for recovery.

The prestige-drama question

The broader question is what this means for prestige, issue-driven dramas in theaters. The Social Reckoning had everything the old Hollywood playbook says should work: an Oscar-winning filmmaker, an acclaimed cast and a story tied to real-world events that dominated headlines. But the theatrical audience of 2026 has been brutally selective, and without a compelling reason to leave the couch, even well-reviewed prestige films are struggling. Analysts increasingly argue that films like this one are better suited to streaming, where they can find an audience over time rather than being judged on a single weekend.

For Sony, the weekend is a reminder that a strong year can contain multitudes. The studio will close 2026 with one of the most profitable films ever made and one of its most conspicuous failures. For Sorkin, it is a humbling chapter: the writer-director who once made Facebook the subject of an instant classic has now made it the subject of a cautionary tale about what happens when prestige meets indifference.
