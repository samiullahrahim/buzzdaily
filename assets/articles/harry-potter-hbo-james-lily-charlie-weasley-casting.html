HBO's Harry Potter Series Casts James and Lily Potter — and Finally Gives Charlie Weasley His Moment

BuzzDaily Staff · Entertainment Desk
· 28 October 2026 · 7 min read
Photo: Oxyman · CC BY 2.5 · via Wikimedia Commons · The Platform 9¾ sign at King's Cross station, London, the fictional departure point of the Hogwarts Express · Free licence — full author and licence details on the Commons file page (file photo)

LOS ANGELES — The family at the heart of Harry Potter is now complete. HBO announced on Tuesday, 27 October, that Hannah Chinn will play Lily Potter and Jasper Talbot will play James Potter in its upcoming Harry Potter television series — and, in the detail that readers of the books have waited decades to see, Harry Gilby has been cast as Charlie Weasley, the dragon-loving older brother the original films never gave a single scene.

The first season, titled “Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone”, premieres on HBO on Christmas Day, 25 December 2026. Dominic McLaughlin leads the series as Harry Potter, with Alastair Stout as Ron Weasley and Arabella Stanton as Hermione Granger. Around them stands one of the most decorated adult ensembles in recent television: John Lithgow as Albus Dumbledore, Janet McTeer as Minerva McGonagall, Paapa Essiedu as Severus Snape and Nick Frost as Rubeus Hagrid. With two months still to go before the premiere, HBO is filling in the faces around its young leads — and Tuesday's names are among the most significant yet.

THE PARENTS HARRY NEVER KNEW

James and Lily Potter are murdered when Harry is a baby, before the story has properly begun — and yet few characters shape the series more. Everything Harry learns about himself arrives filtered through their absence: the parents he glimpses in the Mirror of Erised, standing behind him as the family he should have had; the memories and loyalties of the adults who knew them; and the flashbacks that slowly piece together who they were and what their deaths set in motion. Casting them is not a formality. Whoever plays James and Lily must carry the emotional weight of the entire saga in what may amount to a handful of scenes.

In the eight films, the roles belonged to Adrian Rawlins as James and Geraldine Somerville as Lily, who gave the Potters a warm, ghostly presence across a decade of movies. Chinn and Talbot now inherit that task for television, with one advantage the film actors never had: time. A season built around a single book can return to the past, and linger there, in a way a two-hour film never could.

CHARLIE WEASLEY, AT LAST

If the Potters are the emotional casting, Charlie Weasley is the headline for book readers. Ron's older brother — a dragonologist who works with dragons in Romania — is a vivid presence in the novels: a former Gryffindor Quidditch captain, stocky and fearless, whose career carried him about as far from the Burrow as a Weasley can go. Yet across the original eight films, Charlie was never played by an actor in an actual scene. His entire screen existence amounted to a single appearance in a family photograph in “Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban” — present, technically, and easy to miss entirely.

Gilby's casting corrects one of the films' most frequently noted omissions, and its timing is telling. Charlie barely figures in the events of the first book at all, so casting him now — two months before a season adapting “Philosopher's Stone” — reads as a statement of intent about the seasons to come, and about how completely this production plans to populate its world from the very start.

THREE NAMES TO LEARN

None of Tuesday's three arrives as a household name, and that appears to be rather the point. Chinn's most visible credit to date is an appearance as a television news anchor in “The Conjuring: Last Rites”. Talbot has appeared in “Broadchurch”, the acclaimed British crime drama. Gilby brings the longest résumé of the three, with roles in “The Last Kingdom” and “Boarders” — and a certain symmetry for fantasy readers, having played a young J.R.R. Tolkien, the other cornerstone author of British fantasy, in the 2019 biopic “Tolkien”.

For all three, Harry Potter will be by far the highest-profile project of their careers. The pattern matches the casting of the young leads themselves, and of a production content to set celebrated names — Lithgow, McTeer, Frost — alongside performers most viewers will be meeting for the very first time.

A CAST STILL TAKING SHAPE

Tuesday's announcement is the latest in a steady procession of casting news. Other recently announced additions include Kit Harington as Gilderoy Lockhart, Rafe Spall as Arthur Weasley, Billy Barratt as Tom Riddle, Lenny Rush as Dobby, Molly Hewitt-Richards as Moaning Myrtle and Jasper Ambrose as Colin Creevey. One name, however, remains conspicuously unannounced: Voldemort. HBO has yet to reveal who will play the Dark Lord, and the longer that silence lasts, the more the eventual announcement will dominate the run-up to the premiere.

As reported by TheWrap, the series' logline follows Harry as he receives his Hogwarts letter and sets out on the path that will lead him to face the darkest wizard of all — a reminder that, for all the ensemble casting still to come, this remains one boy's story at its centre.

ONE BOOK PER SEASON, ONE ANNOUNCEMENT AT A TIME

The series is being made as a faithful, book-by-book adaptation — one novel per season — rather than a compressed retelling of the films' story. That structure is precisely what makes castings like these possible: characters the films could only sketch, or were forced to cut altogether, can be restored when the story is given a full season in which to breathe. The staggered announcements, meanwhile, are doing quiet work of their own. Each new name restarts the conversation, and with a Christmas Day premiere fixed on the calendar, HBO has effectively turned its own cast list into a two-month countdown.

For American audiences, the series is HBO's flagship holiday launch, the kind of Christmas Day premiere reserved for the biggest title on a network's slate. For British audiences, it is something more personal: the homecoming of the country's most successful modern story, played by a largely British cast. Both are being offered the same promise — the books, all of them, this time.

WHAT TO WATCH

Analysis — the deepest gain of a faithful adaptation is not length for its own sake; it is texture. The films were masterpieces of compression, but compression has a cost, and it was paid by exactly the characters named on Tuesday: parents reduced to apparitions, a brother reduced to a photograph. A series that restores James and Lily as living figures in flashback, and lets the Weasley family feel on screen as large as it feels on the page, is not padding the story — it is recovering parts of it that a generation of readers has always carried and never seen.

Two questions will follow the production all the way to Christmas. How much of James and Lily's own story will the first season actually tell, when the first book itself reveals them only in fragments — and will the show trust those fragments, or reach further into their past? And will Charlie Weasley appear in person, burns, dragon-hide gloves and all, or reach his family the way he so often does in the early books: by reputation, by letter, by owl post? HBO, on the evidence of this week, understands precisely which details its audience has spent twenty-five years waiting to see. The countdown to 25 December is now a family affair.
