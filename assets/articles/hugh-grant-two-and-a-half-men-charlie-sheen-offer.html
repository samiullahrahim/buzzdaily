Hugh Grant Turned Down $750,000 Per Episode to Replace Charlie Sheen on ‘Two and a Half Men’, Chuck Lorre Reveals

BuzzDaily Staff · Entertainment Desk
· 29 October 2026 · 7 min read
Photo: Kurt Kulac · CC BY-SA 3.0 · via Wikimedia Commons · Hugh Grant at the Wetten, dass..? show in Graz, Austria, in 2014 · Free licence — full author and licence details on the Commons file page (file photo)

LOS ANGELES — It is one of the great what-ifs of modern sitcom history, and it has stayed untold for fifteen years. In his new memoir ‘Sitcom’, published on 13 October with an excerpt in Vanity Fair, Two and a Half Men co-creator Chuck Lorre reveals that when Charlie Sheen was fired from the hit comedy in 2011, the first man CBS and Warner Bros turned to was not Ashton Kutcher. It was Hugh Grant — the British star of Four Weddings and a Funeral and Notting Hill — and the offer put to him was, by any measure, extraordinary: $750,000 per episode, plus a four-day workweek, to become the new lead of one of the biggest comedies on television.

Grant was 51 at the time, and the number tells its own story about how badly the network and the studio wanted him. Sheen, the man he would have replaced, had been making about $1.25 million per episode. Grant was not being offered Sheen’s money, but he was being offered a place close to the top of the television pay scale — and he was being offered it as a film actor who had never headlined an American sitcom, and who, as Lorre’s account makes clear, had serious reservations about what the job would actually involve.

THE SCRAMBLE AFTER SHEEN

Lorre’s account begins in the wreckage of 2011. Sheen’s firing left CBS and Warner Bros holding an enormously lucrative series with no leading man, and then-CBS chairman Les Moonves pushed to keep the show going rather than bring it to an end. That decision set off the casting scramble Lorre now describes: a search for an actor big enough to walk into a hit series mid-run and carry it, conducted under pressure and, on the evidence of the Grant offer, at close to any price. The prize was not a pilot or a promise. It was the lead role in a proven success, with the audience already built in.

A CHARACTER WRITTEN AROUND GRANT

This was no speculative phone call. Lorre and his co-creator Lee Aronsohn sat down and wrote scenes for a new character designed specifically around Grant — in Lorre’s description, “very much like a Hugh Grant type of character”. The writing came before any contract: the producers had already begun reshaping one of the most successful comedies on television around the persona of the actor they hoped would save it. Anyone who knows Grant’s screen image from Four Weddings and a Funeral and Notting Hill can picture the figure Lorre and Aronsohn were writing toward, and how different a Two and a Half Men built around that figure would have felt from the one viewers knew.

THE LIVE AUDIENCE PROBLEM

The sticking point, when it came, was not the money, the billing or the writing. It was the audience. Two and a Half Men was performed in front of a live studio audience, and that was the element Grant could not get past. Lorre’s response, as he tells it in ‘Sitcom’, was to offer to remove the obstacle altogether: eliminate the live audience and use canned laughter instead. It is a remarkable concession for a producer to put in print — the format of a hit show placed on the negotiating table to accommodate one actor’s discomfort — and Lorre is the first to see it that way. “Clearly I have no moral compass,” he jokes in the memoir.

PEN IN HAND, THEN GONE

For a moment, it nearly worked. Lorre writes that Grant appeared close to signing — that he had a pen in hand and was about to sign — and then, abruptly, he walked out of the room. What followed was silence. Grant became, in Lorre’s account, “unreachable”, and it was left to a representative to inform the producers that Grant was passing on the role. The deal that had been offered, written for and all but signed died in that doorway, and the scramble had to begin again with the show’s future still unresolved.

‘HE JUST BAILED’

Lorre has not softened his account of that day in the years since, and he repeated it plainly in interviews around the memoir’s publication. “When it came time to sign a contract and proceed, he bailed. He just bailed,” he told E! News. “I’ve never spoken with him since that time.” Fifteen years on, the striking thing in the telling is less bitterness than bafflement: the offer was real, the character was written, the format itself had proved negotiable — and the answer, when it finally came through a representative, was no.

NOT ABOUT THE MONEY

If the money was not the obstacle, what was? Lorre’s own conclusion, offered to PEOPLE, is that the decision came down to Grant’s career priorities rather than the size of the fee. “The money was secondary to his needs and demands as an actor and as an artist,” he said. Read alongside the live-audience sticking point, the picture that emerges is of an actor who looked carefully at what the job would require of him, in a format he did not want, and decided that no episodic fee — however large — made it the right work. The four-day week and the $750,000 were answers to questions Grant, in the end, was not asking.

GRANT’S OWN VERSION

Grant, for his part, has acknowledged the offer in public exactly once, and briefly. Speaking to Howard Stern in 2016, he confirmed that the deal placed in front of him had been enormous. “It was stratospheric,” he said. “I had no idea how much TV was worth here.” It is, characteristically, nearly all he has ever said on the subject: an admission that American network television money astonished him, offered with no public second-guessing of the decision to walk away from it, then or since.

THE ROLE THAT WENT TO KUTCHER

The scramble ended, as viewers know, with Ashton Kutcher. Cast as Walden Schmidt from season nine, Kutcher appeared in 84 episodes and earned a reported $63 million from the role Grant had declined, while Jon Cryer — the show’s constant through every upheaval — won his second Emmy. The series Moonves had insisted on saving ran on with its new lead, and the near-miss with Grant became one of those casting stories Hollywood keeps to itself. It stayed that way until, fifteen years later, the man who made the offer decided to publish it in a memoir.

Analysis — there is a temptation to file this under television trivia, but Lorre’s account is really a story about leverage, and about the moment it evaporates. In 2011, CBS and Warner Bros needed a star more than any star needed them, and the Grant offer shows what that looks like in practice: the fee, the four-day week, a character written to order, even the live audience itself placed on the table. Grant’s walkout is the other half of the lesson. The only person in that room who could afford to leave it was the one who did — pen in hand, unreachable afterwards, and, if Lorre’s reading is right, entirely clear that the money was never the point.
